# A practical guide to making

Making is not a performance of already knowing. It is a repeatable way to turn a question into something you can test, discuss, improve, and share.

## 1. Find the real question

Write down what you are trying to change. Keep it concrete.

- “I need to hold this sensor at a fixed angle” is easier to act on than “I need a robotics solution.”
- “This chair joint moves when it should not” is a better repair brief than “the chair is broken.”
- “A user should be able to feel this pattern” gives an accessibility project something testable.

## 2. Find people before perfect answers

Show the idea early. A mentor may know the machine. A peer may know the material. A researcher may know the measurement. A community partner may know whether the problem is worth solving in the first place.

Useful conversations are part of the build, not a delay before it.

## 3. Choose the smallest useful test

Do not begin with the final object unless the final object is tiny. Build the version that answers the most important unknown with the least time and material.

| Unknown | Small test |
| --- | --- |
| Will the pieces fit? | Print or cut only the joint. |
| Is the material suitable? | Test a labelled scrap. |
| Can a person understand the interaction? | Make a paper or cardboard mock-up. |
| Does the circuit logic work? | Prototype one input and one output. |
| Is the repair method sound? | Test on a non-critical area or matching offcut. |

## 4. Train for the process

Complete fire and safety training, then the machine-specific primer. Read the linked manual before you arrive so supervised time can focus on the parts that are difficult to learn from a page.

Never let a deadline turn into permission to skip training, approved materials, supervision, or protective equipment.

## 5. Build, observe, change one thing

Make the first version. Record what happened. Change one important variable where possible, then test again. Iteration is easier to understand when every version is not a completely different project wearing the same name.

Failure is useful when it becomes evidence. It is merely expensive when nobody writes down what happened.

## 6. Document enough to be generous

For each useful version, capture:

1. the problem or goal;
2. the people who contributed;
3. tools, materials, files, and settings;
4. photos of the build and test;
5. what worked and what did not;
6. safety notes or material restrictions; and
7. the next thing you would try.

Documentation is how a project joins the network. It lets another maker reproduce the useful part, avoid the same dead end, or take the idea somewhere you did not expect.

## 7. Share the bench

Return tools, label stored work, remove waste, reset machine settings, and leave the area ready for the next person. Credit collaborators and sources. Offer help when you can do so safely, and say “I do not know” early enough to find someone who does.
